<p class="article-lead">A lung scan can reveal a suspicious spot. Getting enough tissue to identify it is a different problem. Oxford's new robotic bronchoscopy service helps clinicians reach difficult spots through the airways, bringing a specialist procedure closer to local patients.</p>

## Quick read

- **Already in use:** Oxford University Hospitals announced its Ion service at John Radcliffe Hospital on 17 August 2026, with patients already treated.
- **A doctor controls it:** the robot helps steer and hold a thin catheter. It does not decide whether someone has cancer.
- **Evidence is narrower than the headline:** a randomised trial found robotic sampling met its noninferiority target against another guided bronchoscopy system. That is not proof it is better.
- **The caveat:** some nodules need monitoring rather than biopsy. When sampling is needed, the best route depends on the patient and the nodule; complications and inconclusive results remain possible.

## What changed in Oxford

The new service covers Oxfordshire and the wider Thames Valley. OUH expects around **150 patients a year** to benefit who would previously have travelled to specialist centres in London. That is expected activity, not a year's completed procedures. The hospital also anticipates fewer repeat investigations, but its announcement does not provide comparative local results. [OUH announcement](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/news/articles/2460/)

This is a local rollout of an existing technology, not a robot invented this month. Its practical value is less dramatic and more useful: another hospital can offer a difficult diagnostic procedure.

## How the robot helps

Bronchoscopy means passing an instrument through the airway to examine or sample the lung. Ion adds a steerable catheter with an outer diameter of **3.5 mm**. A plan built from a CT scan guides the route; fibre-optic sensing tracks the catheter's shape. The clinician steers it using controls and can hold it in position while taking samples. [Intuitive's technical explanation](https://www.intuitive.com/en-us/products-and-services/ion/shape-sensing)

The CT roadmap is not the same as confirming the target during the procedure. Imaging and clinical judgement still matter. The RELIANT trial, for example, used ultrasound and made additional imaging available during robotic procedures. [RELIANT methods](https://academic.oup.com/ajrccm/article/211/9/1644/8300624)

## Three ways to reach a lung nodule

The choice is not simply "robot or old-fashioned medicine". Nonrobotic navigation also exists, and a needle through the chest can be appropriate for some targets.

| Approach | Route to the tissue | Key distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CT-guided needle biopsy | Through the skin and chest wall | Imaging guides a needle to the target; does not need an airway route |
| Nonrobotic guided bronchoscopy | Through the airways | Uses electromagnetic tracking without robotic catheter control |
| Robotic guided bronchoscopy | Through the airways | Adds shape sensing, robotic steering and position holding |

Route descriptions: [NHS biopsy guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lung-cancer/diagnosis/) and [RELIANT platform comparison](https://academic.oup.com/ajrccm/article/211/9/1644/8300624).

A **nodule is a small spot, not a diagnosis of cancer**. Many are benign. Some are better followed with scans because an invasive test would add risk without enough benefit. Size, position and the person's health affect what can safely be sampled. An inconclusive biopsy is not an all-clear. [NHS nodule guidance](https://www.nth.nhs.uk/resources/pulmonary-nodules/)

## Two trials, two different questions

### 1. Does robotic sampling outperform another guided bronchoscopy system?

The 2025 **RELIANT** trial compared Ion with Medtronic's Illumisite at one US academic centre. Its main measure was **diagnostic yield**: obtaining tissue that explained the lesion.

| RELIANT outcome | Ion (robotic) | Illumisite |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Diagnostic tissue obtained | 158 / 203 | 157 / 208 |
| Diagnostic yield | 77.8% | 75.5% |

The trial met its noninferiority criterion: the results excluded a disadvantage larger than its prespecified 10 percentage-point margin. **It did not establish robotic superiority.** The imaging setups also differed, so this was a comparison of clinical workflows, not just two steering mechanisms. [RELIANT paper](https://academic.oup.com/ajrccm/article/211/9/1644/8300624)

### 2. How does an airway biopsy compare with a needle through the chest?

The 2025 **VERITAS** trial studied selected patients with 10-30 mm nodules at seven US centres. It measured whether the diagnosis remained correct through 12 months, not just whether lesion tissue was obtained.

| VERITAS outcome | Guided airway | Chest needle |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Confirmed diagnostic accuracy | 79.0%<br />94 / 119 | 73.6%<br />81 / 110 |
| Pneumothorax | 3.3%<br />4 / 121 | 28.3%<br />32 / 113 |

Counts show patients with the outcome / patients assessed.

The accuracy result met noninferiority, not superiority. Pneumothorax was less frequent in the bronchoscopy group. The denominators differ because the accuracy analysis required follow-up. [VERITAS paper](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2414059), [accessible abstract](https://pure.johnshopkins.edu/en/publications/navigational-bronchoscopy-or-transthoracic-needle-biopsy-for-lung/)

**This was not an Ion trial.** Its bronchoscopy arm used electromagnetic Illumisite navigation. Medtronic helped fund the study. The result supports an airway approach for suitable patients; it cannot be advertised as a robot-specific reduction in complications. [Platform identification](https://news.medtronic.com/VERITAS-study-shows-Medtronic-fluoroscopic-navigation-platform-offers-a-safer-lung-biopsy-experience-and-the-same-diagnostic-yield-as-CT-guided-biopsy)

Pneumothorax means air leaking into the space around the lung, which can make it collapse. The lower number is not zero risk. Robotic bronchoscopy also has risks, including bleeding and complications of general anaesthesia. [NHS risk explanation](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/lung-cancer/diagnosis/), [Ion safety information](https://www.intuitive.com/en-us/products-and-services/ion/shape-sensing)

## What would prove the local value

For Oxford, I would watch four measures rather than the number of robots installed:

| Measure | What it would tell patients |
| --- | --- |
| Time from referral to a usable diagnosis | Whether answers actually arrive sooner |
| Repeat biopsies and complications | Whether the whole pathway is easier, not just the first procedure |
| Travel avoided | Whether specialist care is genuinely closer to home |
| Total cost per completed diagnosis | Whether the service can be sustained, including staff, imaging and repeat tests |

These are evaluation criteria, not results Oxford has published in its launch announcement. Neither trial above establishes a survival benefit from this local rollout.

The useful achievement is a specialist service reaching more people. Whether the robot is the best tool for an individual case remains a clinical decision, not something a product label or a single percentage can settle.
