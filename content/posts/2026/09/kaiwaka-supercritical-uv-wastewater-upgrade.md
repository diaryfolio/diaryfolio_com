<p class="article-lead">A small New Zealand wastewater plant needed more disinfection capacity during heavy rain, but had little room to expand. Kaiwaka added a compact ultraviolet stage that spreads wastewater into a thin sheet before exposing it to UV light.</p>

## Quick read

- **Operating upgrade:** Kaipara District Council says the Kaiwaka system began work in October 2025 and was completed in early 2026.
- **Capacity claim:** the council says Supercritical UV, or SCUV, triples the plant's ability to disinfect higher wet-weather flows.
- **Cost:** the project cost about **NZD 227,000**, compared with early alternative estimates above **NZD 1 million**.
- **The caveat:** three times the disinfection flow capacity does not mean three times cleaner water. Kaiwaka's before-and-after pathogen, energy and operating-cost data are not public.

## The bottleneck Kaiwaka addressed

Kaiwaka uses an oxidation-pond treatment scheme. These plants are simple and relatively cheap to operate, but their performance and hydraulic capacity can be difficult to manage during wet weather. Kaipara's 2021 asset plan identifies inflow and infiltration as a district-wide problem: rain or groundwater enters wastewater networks and raises the flow reaching pumps and treatment plants. [Kaipara wastewater plan, pages 13-15](https://kaipara.govt.nz/uploads/assetmanagementplans/SAMPS%202021/Wastewater%20SAMP%20FINAL%20June%202021.pdf)

The 2026 project did not replace the entire plant. It added capacity at the **disinfection stage**, where treated wastewater is exposed to UV before discharge.

| Council account | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose: cope with higher wet-weather flow | Does not eliminate rainwater entering the network |
| Capacity: three times the previous ability to disinfect wastewater in wet weather | Does not mean three times the whole plant capacity or three times cleaner effluent |
| Capital cost: about NZD 227,000 | Does not include a published lifecycle or future maintenance cost |
| Alternative: early solutions were estimated above NZD 1 million | Not a published like-for-like tender comparison with identical scope |
| Delivery: started October 2025 and completed early 2026 | Does not provide a full year of operating results |

The [council announcement](https://www.kaipara.govt.nz/news/post/1024-Kaiwaka-wastewater-treatment-plant-upgraded) says the site had little room to expand. That makes a modular process useful even before any claim about treatment performance: more equipment can fit into an existing constrained site.

## How thin-sheet UV works

Conventional UV systems often place lamps in a channel or pipe. Light has to travel through the liquid, so turbidity, suspended particles and low UV transmittance can shield microorganisms.

NovoLabs describes a different light-delivery geometry:

1. A module turns the flow into a thin, fast-moving sheet.
2. Low-pressure amalgam UV lamps shine from above rather than sitting in the wastewater.
3. A reflective channel increases light exposure across the shallow liquid layer.
4. Several modules can operate in parallel as flow changes.

[NovoLabs system explanation](https://novolabs.co.nz/scuv-system/)

The word **supercritical** refers to the fast, shallow hydraulic flow used in the reactor. It does not mean the wastewater becomes a supercritical fluid under extreme heat or pressure.

UV is a disinfection step. The US EPA explains that UV energy damages microbial DNA and RNA, reducing the organisms' ability to reproduce. Effectiveness depends on wastewater quality, light intensity, exposure time and reactor design. [EPA UV fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-06/documents/uv.pdf)

| UV can do | UV does not automatically do |
| --- | --- |
| Inactivate susceptible microorganisms at an adequate dose | Remove suspended solids, nitrogen, phosphorus, metals or salt |
| Avoid adding a chemical disinfectant residual | Provide a lasting disinfectant residual after treatment |
| Fit into a compact final treatment stage | Fix upstream hydraulic overload or leaking sewers |

SCUV is designed for liquids where ordinary UV penetration is difficult. A 2025 Water Environment Federation paper describes it as an emerging technology for low-UVT or high-turbidity effluent. That paper supports the technical category, not Kaiwaka's individual performance. [WEFTEC paper record](https://www.accesswater.org/?id=-10118879&webtemplateoverride=true)

## Capacity without guessing the model

NovoLabs sells modular systems from one to ten treatment modules. Its standard catalogue lists maximum nominal flows from 570 to 5,700 cubic metres per day, with lower throughput required for difficult liquids. [NovoLabs specifications](https://novolabs.co.nz/scuv-specifications/)

Kaiwaka's exact model and module count are not stated publicly. Therefore the catalogue cannot be used to calculate this plant's:

- daily flow capacity;
- UV dose;
- electricity consumption;
- lamp count or replacement interval; or
- spare capacity during a module outage.

The same rule applies to other installations. NovoLabs publishes an Ahipara case study with E. coli, suspended-solids and UV-transmittance results. Those figures help explain why councils considered the technology, but they are **Ahipara results, not Kaiwaka results**. [Ahipara case study](https://www.novolabs.co.nz/case-studies/from-oversight-to-impact-rebuilding-trust-with-data-driven-systems-2)

## What the cost comparison means

The difference between about NZD 227,000 and an early estimate above NZD 1 million is substantial. It suggests that adding a compact disinfection stage avoided a larger civil expansion.

It is not enough to claim a permanent saving of roughly NZD 773,000 or more. Early estimates can cover different scopes, and the council has not published tender breakdowns, financing, energy use, lamp replacement or long-term service costs. The defensible statement is narrower: **the delivered capital project cost much less than the early alternative estimate reported by the council**.

## What to measure next

| Measure | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Flow through the SCUV stage during storms | Confirms the claimed wet-weather capacity under actual events |
| E. coli or consent-indicator results before and after UV | Shows disinfection performance, not just hydraulic throughput |
| UV transmittance and suspended solids | Explains how difficult the incoming liquid was to treat |
| Electricity per cubic metre | Tests operating efficiency across dry and wet periods |
| Lamp hours, cleaning and replacements | Captures the maintenance burden |
| Bypasses, alarms and module downtime | Shows whether parallel modules protect service continuity |
| Annualised operating cost | Allows a fair whole-life comparison with the larger alternative |

My assessment is that Kaiwaka is a strong **implementation story**, not yet a performance case study. The council solved a real space and wet-weather constraint with an operating system at a much lower stated capital cost than early alternatives. Publishing a year of flow, microbiology, energy and maintenance data would show whether that value holds in routine operation.
